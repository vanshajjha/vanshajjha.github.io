Pat Cummins!

Ahmedabad, 19 November 2023. World Cup final. Around a lakh of people in blue, and an Indian team that had won all ten of its matches on the way there. As an Indian fan, I'm typing this paragraph through gritted teeth.

Just picture this scene: you're the Australian captain. You win the toss. Conventional wisdom says bat first, put runs on the board and let the pressure do the rest. Rohit Sharma said at the toss that he would have batted. Brad Haddin said he was shocked that Australia didn't.

Cummins chose to bowl.

He wasn't guessing. He had looked at the surface and seen a dry pitch that would help his bowlers in the afternoon. He knew it gets dewy in Ahmedabad at night, and dew makes the ball wet and hard to grip, which makes chasing easier. And three of the four matches at that ground during the tournament had been won by the side batting second.

India raced to 80 for 2 in the first ten overs, and Cummins looked like a man who had overthought it. Then the pitch did exactly what he'd read it would do. India were bowled out for 240. Cummins took 2-34, including Virat Kohli, after which, by his own account, the stadium went "as quiet as a library". Australia wobbled to 47 for 3, then Travis Head made 137 and Australia won by six wickets with seven overs to spare.

So, what won that final?

Not a louder crowd or a better team talk. Cummins read the conditions before he picked his plan. The crowd told him how people felt. The pitch told him how the game would actually be won.

That, in my view, is the difference between an HRBP who starts with the engagement survey and one who starts with the P&L.

## The crowd and the pitch

Here's how a new HRBP's first month usually goes. Week one: induction. Week two: "Let's go through the engagement results." Week three: a heatmap with 14 shades of amber, and an action plan that includes a town hall, a manager toolkit and, inevitably, "improve communication".

None of that is wrong. It's just the crowd noise. It tells you how people feel. It doesn't tell you where the business makes and loses money, which is what your leader lies awake about.

And the expectation from the top is clear. Gartner's CHRO priorities for 2027, released on 6 October 2026, centre on delivering the growth and productivity CEOs want while keeping risk in check. In its July 2026 survey, 67% of CHROs said expectations for employee productivity had gone up. The World Economic Forum's first Chief People Officers Outlook (September 2025) found business acumen was the top-ranked skill for people leaders, and every CPO surveyed placed it, alongside strategic thinking, in their top three.

The crowd isn't happy either. Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2026 puts engaged employees in India at 23%, down from 30% a year earlier. That's worth acting on. But you can't fix it well if you don't know which teams the business can't afford to lose.

AI makes this easier to get wrong, faster. It will summarise 3,000 survey comments in 30 seconds and tell you people want better communication. It always does. What it won't tell you is that the angriest comments came from the team running your biggest client account. That takes a human who has read the account list.

## Why the P&L comes first

In Indian IT services, people aren't one line on the P&L. They're most of it. TCS's fact sheet for the September 2026 quarter, released on 8 October, shows employee costs at 57.2% of revenue, against an operating margin of 24.0%, with a headcount of just under six lakh. When more than half of every rupee earned goes on people, almost every HR decision is a margin decision, whether HR frames it that way or not.

Dave Ulrich has been making this case for years. In a 2019 interview with Roland Berger, he argued that HR's value is measured outside the firm, by customers and investors, not by HR activity. Brian Heger makes the practical version in his *Talent Edge Weekly* newsletter: his August 2025 issue led with a one-pager on framing every talent initiative around the business problem it solves. I'd add one thing for HRBPs in services: you can't frame anything around the business problem until you've read the business's numbers.

## A first 30 days that starts with the pitch

Here's the sequence I'd suggest.

**Week 1: How does this business make money?** Is the revenue time-and-materials, fixed-price or a managed service? Who are the top five clients, and what share of revenue do they bring? Which deals are up for renewal this year?

**Week 2: What are the unit economics?** Revenue per billable person, cost per person, utilisation, bench size and the shape of the pyramid. Sit with your finance partner for an hour. Bring chai. Ask the questions you're embarrassed to ask.

**Week 3: Where does margin leak?** Ask delivery heads where projects go over budget, where they're stretched and which roles take longest to fill.

**Week 4: Now open the survey.** Read it through the business lens. Map the scores against the accounts and roles you now know matter most.

## A worked example

These numbers are hypothetical.

Take a 300-person delivery unit. Utilisation is 80%, so 240 people are billable. Each billable person brings in ₹30 lakh a year, so revenue is ₹72 crore. Average cost per employee is ₹14 lakh, so people cost ₹42 crore, about 58% of revenue.

The survey says engagement is down across the unit. The survey-first HRBP proposes a unit-wide engagement programme.

The P&L-first HRBP sees three things. One point of utilisation is three billable people, or about ₹90 lakh of revenue a year. The 60 people not billing cost about ₹8.4 crore a year. And the lowest scores sit in the team that runs the account bringing in 40% of revenue, which renews next year.

So the plan changes. Fix staffing and manager support on that one account first. Work with delivery on moving bench people into billable roles. Then run the broader engagement work. Same survey. A very different first move.

## How to know it's working

Judge yourself on three things:

- **Every HR priority names a P&L line.** If your top three initiatives can't each point to revenue, margin, utilisation or a key account, they're still crowd noise.
- **That line moves.** Track the metric you named, such as utilisation, revenue per person or regretted attrition on key accounts, quarter on quarter.
- **The finance test.** Ask your finance partner to explain your top HR priority and why it matters. If they can, you're partnering. If they look puzzled, start again at week one.

Cummins didn't ignore the crowd. He just knew it wasn't the thing that decided the result.

**PS:** I asked an AI to read a P&L and recommend one HR intervention. It suggested a wellness webinar. Some habits, it seems, are in the training data.
