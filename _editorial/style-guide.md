# Style guide

Built from Vanshaj's own writing (see `samples/`). Updated after each post from the edits he makes to drafts. When in doubt, re-read the samples: match the energy, not the exact phrases.

## Signature move: the cricket analogy
Vanshaj's posts open with a real cricket moment and use it as the spine of the HR argument.
- **Open on the moment, not the lesson.** Start with a name, a scene or a scoreline. The sample opens with just "Ben Stokes!"
- **Put the reader in the captain's shoes.** "Just picture this scene: You have a debutant in your team…" Second person, present tense, stakes made clear.
- **Bring in the doubters.** Pundits, critics or the obvious alternative, so the leader's choice looks like a real decision with a real downside.
- **Then the payoff.** "Fast forward to the second innings, and behold!" The turnaround is told with colour.
- **Pivot with a question.** "So, what sparked this turnaround?" This marks the move from cricket to the workplace.
- **State the principle plainly.** One paragraph that names the leadership or HR lesson.
- **Sign off with a personal, fan-ish aside.** "PS: I still think we will win this series." A little humour, a real person behind the post.

## Using the analogy well (rules for the agent)
- **Every cricket fact must be accurate and sourced:** match, venue, date, figures, who said what. Check scorecards (ESPNcricinfo, ICC, BCCI) and cite them. A wrong scoreline destroys credibility with exactly the readers this blog targets.
- **The analogy is the hook, not the whole post.** On the blog, cricket takes roughly the first 20–30% of the words. The rest goes from the lesson into business and HR substance: what it costs, how to diagnose it, what an HRBP actually does, how to measure it.
- **Push past the inspirational lesson to the commercial one.** "Back your people and they'll deliver" is where the sample ends. The blog version must go further. What does backing talent cost in time and money? What does churning through debutants cost? How would a manager tell a Hartley from someone who genuinely isn't working out? This is what separates a commercial HRBP from a motivational post.
- **Rotate the moment, don't force it.** Use cricket in most posts, but only when the parallel is genuinely tight. If none fits, open with a sharp business scene instead, and never stretch an analogy until it breaks. Vary the eras, formats (Tests, ODIs, T20, IPL) and teams. Avoid reusing the same player within a few months.
- **Keep it accessible.** Explain any cricket term a non-fan HR leader wouldn't know, in a few words.

## Humour (Vanshaj's request, Oct 2026: "more humour")
Vanshaj wants posts that make HR leaders smile and then think. The model is the best of LinkedIn workplace humour: short, specific, deadpan, and always carrying a real point. See `samples/humour-references.md` for the techniques, taken from writers he admires.

### The recurring theme: humans still matter in the AI story
In most posts, and always in AI or HR-tech posts, include at least one light beat showing that AI does the task but humans supply judgement, context, courage or accountability. Make it land as a joke or an ironic twist, never a lecture. Patterns that work:
- **The reversal.** The AI does exactly what it was asked, and that's the problem: it flags the person who commissioned it, optimises the wrong metric, or "solves" attrition by recommending nobody be hired.
- **Speed vs sense.** The AI drafts the performance review in 4 seconds. The manager still needs the courage to have the conversation, and that takes three weeks.
- **The consequence lands on the wrong person.** As in the CEO-agent joke, where the specialist gets fired, not the CEO. This is power dynamics, which HR knows better than anyone.
Don't force it into every post. If the topic has no AI angle, a human-judgement beat is enough (the dashboard nobody reads, the policy nobody follows).

### Techniques (use 2–3 per post, not all of them)
1. **Deadpan setup, twist ending.** Tell the setup straight, in flat corporate language, then flip it in the last line. Don't explain the joke.
2. **Specific numbers make it funnier.** "After 9 months of data and 20 hours of work…" is funnier than "after a lot of work". Precision sells the absurdity.
3. **The exaggerated hypothetical.** Push a real workplace habit to the absurd, like Ansh Jain imagining GenZ colleagues reporting an office fire by casual text. The reader laughs because they recognise it.
4. **Mimic the voice.** Quote what the CFO, the GenZ analyst, the anxious first-time manager or the vendor demo would actually say, in their style.
5. **Self-deprecation.** Vanshaj laughs at himself first, the way Ansh Jain admits feeling like "an uncle" for insisting on phone calls. It earns the right to make the point and keeps the humour kind.
6. **The rule of three.** List three things where the third breaks the pattern (e.g. three generations, three dashboard metrics, three excuses).
7. **The mock rant.** One line in CAPS or a one-line paragraph of exasperation, used once at most.
8. **Cricket humour.** Gentle fan jokes (the batter who reviews everything with DRS, the captain who sets a field nobody understands) to sit alongside the analogy.

### Placement and dose
- **Hook:** the opening can be a joke or a funny scene instead of, or alongside, the cricket moment.
- **One mid-post beat**, usually where the business problem is introduced ("Open any HR review and you'll find 42 metrics. The CEO reads one: the slide number, to see how many are left.").
- **PS:** the PS is now always a light, funny line.
- **Roughly one laugh per 300 words.** The analysis, numbers and recommendations stay precise. Humour opens the door; substance has to walk through it.

### Guardrails (non-negotiable)
- **Punch up or at yourself, never down.** Joke about leaders, vendors, dashboards, meetings, AI hype, HR's own habits. Never mock people with less power: laid-off employees, junior staff, people on PIPs.
- **Never joke about** layoffs, mental health, harassment or employee-relations cases, protected characteristics, or any current or former employer.
- **Generational humour stays affectionate.** Include your own generation in the joke (as Ansh Jain does with millennials), and never make a group the butt of the whole post.
- **Invented anecdotes must be obviously jokes or clearly hypothetical.** Write "Picture this…", "I'm told a CEO once…" or frame it as a meme-style story. Never present a made-up story as something that really happened to Vanshaj or a real company. Facts and statistics still need sources, even inside a funny post.
- **Write original jokes.** Learn the techniques from `samples/humour-references.md`; never reuse other writers' lines or premises as Vanshaj's own.
- **If a joke needs explaining, cut it.**

## Voice
- First person, enthusiastic, conversational and **funny**: a fan who happens to be an HR professional, talking to peers over chai. Wry, observational and self-aware, never sarcastic about people.
- Rhetorical questions answered immediately: "Did this make Ben Stokes, the leader, flinch? No!"
- Vivid, sporty imagery in the cricket part ("smashing him all over the park", "spinning the ball with absolute venom"). The business part is calmer and more precise. Keep the colour in the story and the clarity in the analysis.
- Exclamations are part of the voice, but use at most 2–3 per post.
- Indian context by default (₹, lakh/crore, Indian companies and labour market).

## Polish (tightening Vanshaj's natural voice without changing it)
- Keep sentences to one idea. The sample's longest sentences (e.g. "show the world what he can help his team truly achieve") lose force. Split or trim them.
- Avoid repeating a word within a paragraph: "ultimately" appears three times in two paragraphs in the sample, and "unleash" twice.
- Use cricket terms precisely: "figures of 7-62", not "a figure of".
- Short paragraphs (1–4 sentences). One-line paragraphs are fine for punch.

## Structure for a blog post (900–1,300 words)
1. **The cricket moment** (200–300 words): scene, stakes, doubters, payoff.
2. **The pivot question** and the principle, in one short paragraph.
3. **The business problem**: why this matters commercially, with sourced evidence.
4. **The HRBP playbook**: what to diagnose, what to do and who to involve, with one worked example using clearly hypothetical numbers and a `[YOUR EXAMPLE]` placeholder where Vanshaj's experience fits.
5. **How to measure it**: two or three metrics a leader would care about.
6. **The PS**: a short, funny line, ideally a human-vs-AI or cricket twist. Then sources and the disclosure line.

Humour runs through the structure: a joke or funny scene in the hook, one beat when the business problem appears, and the PS.
